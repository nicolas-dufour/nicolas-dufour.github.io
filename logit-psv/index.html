Rethinking Neural Thickets Without Weight Perturbations

Nicolas Dufour1, Elouan Gardès2,3, Amil Dravid4, Patrick Pérez5*, Loic Landrieu3

1Kyutai  ·  2Meta FAIR  ·  3LIGM, CNRS, Univ Gustave Eiffel, ENPC, Institut Polytechnique de Paris  ·  4UC Berkeley  ·  5Google DeepMind
*Work done while at Kyutai.

PaperCodeBibTeX

Neural Thickets perturbs every weight of a language model, keeps the copies that score best on a small labeled set, and has them vote. We show that a random bias on the output logits, with all other weights unchanged, matches or exceeds these gains on a diverse set of tasks. We also extend the method to non-generative models such as DINOv3.

The logits are enough

A random bias on the output logits matches full-weight perturbation: MATH-500 goes from 63.3 to 81.3 on OLMo-3-7B-Instruct, against 74.7.

Weight noise acts through the logits

To first order, a weight perturbation is a shift of the logits. At the noise scale used, this reproduces 89.7% of argmax changes.

It carries over to vision

Under domain shift, perturbing the logits of a DINOv3 probe adds 6.2 points of worst-region accuracy on FMoW; perturbing the weights adds 1.0.

1 · BackgroundNeural Thickets and perturb, select, vote

Gan and Isola add Gaussian noise to all the weights of a pretrained language model, score each noisy copy on 200 examples, keep the best ones and take a majority vote over their answers. With a few thousand copies and K = 50, this improves OLMo-3-7B-Instruct by about 20 points on Countdown and 13 on MATH-500, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct by 52.8 points on Countdown, at a small fraction of the cost of fine-tuning.

They explain the gain geometrically: the pretrained weights would lie in a dense region of task-specific models, a thicket, that random perturbations can reach. We keep the procedure and test whether the gain depends on perturbing the weights.

We refer to the procedure as perturb, select, vote (PSV). It is defined by what is perturbed, a labeled selection set and a voting rule. Neural Thickets perturbs every weight: each member is θ + σε with ε ~ 𝒩(0, I). The figure below runs it on a toy question.

PSV with noise on every weight (schematic). Left: each copy of the model traced through the 32 decoder blocks. Middle: the validation accuracy of each copy, then, for the kept copies, the shift of their logits toward the correct answer on one test question. Right: the vote of the kept copies and, for comparison, the vote of all 24. The question and the scores are illustrative.

2 · Localized perturbationsOne block is enough

If the gain required a coordinated change of all the weights, perturbing a small part of the network should lose most of it. On Countdown with OLMo-3-7B-Instruct (K = 50), the unperturbed model scores 66.6% and full-weight PSV 90.8%. Perturbing a single decoder block, with the other 31 fixed, improves on the base model for every block; the later blocks come close to 90.8%, and the last one reaches 91.8%.

The same holds for single matrices of the first block. Components that write into the residual stream recover the full gain, the value and feed-forward input projections 96 to 99% of it, and the query and key path, which acts through the attention softmax, 85 to 89%. Qwen2.5 from 0.5B to 3B shows the same pattern.

3 · Perturbing only the outputLogit PSV

Section 2 shows that the perturbation can be much smaller than the full network. We therefore look for the minimal perturbation that can still explain the gains: we keep the network fixed and perturb only its output. Each member draws one Gaussian vector over the vocabulary and adds it to the logits, at every decoding step and for every input:

ℓi = ℓ + σ εi,   εi ~ 𝒩(0, I)

This amounts to perturbing the output-layer bias, one value per vocabulary token, with all other weights unchanged. Selection and voting are unchanged. We call this logit PSV.

Adding a bias to the logits multiplies the probability of each token by a fixed factor. The toy example below applies one such bias to eight validation questions. Most members do no better than the unperturbed model. A few fix several questions at once, for example by lowering a token that the model predicts too often, and selection keeps those.

Toy example. Made-up logits for one decoding step, the first digit of the answer, on eight validation questions. Bars show the member's next-token probabilities; ticks show those of the unperturbed model. Each member draws one bias and adds it to the logits of all eight questions; the numbers under the bias are the factors it applies to each token's probability before renormalization. Selection keeps the members with the highest validation accuracy, whose accuracy on test questions can be lower.

Schematic. The same procedure in every row: members are placed at their validation accuracy, the best 8 of 24 are kept, and they answer a test question that the unperturbed model gets wrong (36 instead of 26). The paper uses 1000 to 5000 members and keeps 50.

Logit PSV matches or exceeds full-weight PSV on most reasoning tasks. Across four models it is more accurate on 13 of 15 reasoning cells and within 0.6 points on the other two; it is usually slightly lower on MBPP and ROCStories. Most of these differences are within test-set noise.

Selection and voting

In terms of logits, PSV combines three known components: the perturbations make the members' outputs differ, selection on a validation set keeps the members whose shift helps across many examples (similar to verifier-guided selection, but done once before test time), and voting is self-consistency over members. To isolate selection, we compare with temperature-tuned majority voting (TT-MV), which samples the unperturbed model at T = 0.7 and votes without selection. On strong models, sampling and voting account for much of the gain: on MATH-500 with OLMo-3-7B-Instruct, TT-MV reaches 77.7%, against 74.7% for full-weight PSV with 1000 members and 80.7% with 5000. On weaker models selection matters: on Countdown with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, TT-MV reaches at most 34.3% (at T = 0.12), below full-weight PSV at 37.4%.

4 · First-order analysisWeight noise acts through the logits

A perturbed model changes its answer only through its logits. For the perturbation used by Neural Thickets, noise σε on every weight, the first-order change of the logits is σJ(x)ε: the change made by each block enters the residual stream, these changes add up, and the unembedding maps their sum to the logits. Adding this shift as a bias on the logits, with all weights unchanged, gives the same logits to first order. Logit PSV samples such a bias directly.

Schematic, for one input and one decoding step. Route 1 perturbs every weight; route 2 adds route 1's first-order logit shift as an output bias. Shifts are drawn per unit of σ. Agreement is measured on OLMo-3-7B-Instruct for Countdown: the fraction of argmax changes under weight noise that the first-order logits reproduce.

At σ = 10⁻³, the scale used in the experiments, the first-order logits reproduce 89.7% of the argmax changes of the model with every weight perturbed. Agreement decreases as σ grows.

The match holds for each input separately. J(x) depends on the input, so a weight perturbation shifts the logits differently on each input, whereas a logit bias applies the same shift to all of them. Logit PSV uses this simpler form, with a random bias, and still matches the gains.

5 · VisionVision under domain shift

Vision backbones are usually adapted with a linear probe, and a classifier makes a single prediction per image. We define a domain expert as a perturbed backbone whose features give a better probe on a shifted domain. Full-weight PSV perturbs a DINOv3 backbone and fits a probe for each member; logit PSV fits one probe and perturbs its class logits. We use ImageNet as a control and two datasets with a strong shift between training and test, FMoW and Waterbirds.

Sample images. ImageNet (Deng et al., 2009; images from the Imagenette subset), FMoW-WILDS (Christie et al., 2018; Koh et al., 2021) and Waterbirds (Sagawa et al., 2020, built from CUB and Places).

Weight perturbations improve accuracy by at most one point on the three datasets, even with the 6.7B-parameter backbone. Logit PSV has no effect on ImageNet and improves worst-group accuracy by 6.2 points on FMoW and 5.0 on Waterbirds.

Closed-form recalibration

To test whether the gain is a recalibration, we compare with a fixed correction that needs no sampling, selection or voting. On the validation group of interest, let πtrue(c) be the frequency of class c and πpred(c) the frequency with which the probe predicts it. We add to the logit of each class

bc = log πtrue(c) − log πpred(c).

Classes that the probe predicts too often get a negative bias and classes it predicts too rarely a positive one, so the predicted class frequencies move toward the true ones. With two classes, this is the same as moving the decision threshold. On ImageNet, where classes are balanced and there is no shift, logit PSV has no effect and the correction is not applicable. On FMoW the correction recovers 3.7 of the 6.2 points, and on Waterbirds it matches logit PSV (+5.0). The vision gains therefore look like recalibration under distribution shift rather than the discovery of domain experts.

6 · ConclusionConclusion

Neural Thickets showed that a pretrained language model can be improved on a task without training: perturb its weights at random, keep the copies that score best on 200 examples and let them vote. We kept this procedure and changed where the noise goes. Noise in a single decoder block gives about the same gain as noise in every weight: on Countdown with OLMo-3-7B-Instruct, the last block alone reaches 91.8%, against 90.8% for every weight. Noise on the output bias alone, with every other weight unchanged, is more accurate than full-weight PSV on 13 of 15 reasoning cells. The first-order analysis shows why the location matters little: wherever the noise is applied, its leading effect is a shift of the logits.

Under logit PSV, each member is a single vector over the vocabulary. It multiplies the probability of each token by a fixed factor, the same at every decoding step and on every input, and selection keeps the vectors that help on the task. In vision, this reading can be tested directly: a closed-form correction of the class frequencies gives 3.7 of the 6.2 points of logit PSV on FMoW, and the same +5.0 points on Waterbirds. Our results do not exclude useful structure near the pretrained weights; they show that it is not needed for the gains measured here.

Logit PSV is also simpler to run. It is training-free, and since members differ only in the output bias, they share the rest of the network and can be decoded in the same batch, each with its own bias.
